7 Legal Mistakes Threatening Your Business

A Practical Guide for Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) Owners in KSA — Prepared by Attorney Saleh Al-Mohamadi

1 Verbal Agreements — The Hidden Enemy of Your Business

Many business owners in Riyadh continue to finalize deals, partnerships, and supply arrangements verbally. "A gentleman's agreement" is a phrase that costs companies millions of Riyals annually. Verbal agreements are extremely difficult to prove in court, relying entirely on memory and goodwill — both of which vanish rapidly at the first sign of a commercial dispute.

Preventative Tip: Any commercial commitment exceeding 10,000 SAR should be documented in a written B2B contract. Even a simple contract protects you from lawsuits that could cost ten times the original transaction value.

2 Copying Generic Contracts from the Internet

Downloading standard contract templates from generic websites is often worse than having no contract at all. These templates are generic, outdated, and frequently violate Saudi statutory laws regarding termination, liability caps, and arbitration. What fits a construction company will not fit a software developer, and Saudi regulations differ significantly from Egyptian or Emirati laws.

Preventative Tip: Every commercial contract requires customized drafting to match your specific sector and governing Saudi laws. Investing in custom drafting secures your business against future litigations.

3 Neglecting Contract Termination & Exit Clauses

Most companies focus heavily on pricing and service delivery terms, neglecting the exit clause — which is the most critical safeguard in any agreement. Without a clear termination clause, you may remain bound to a supplier or client for years even if they fail to fulfill their obligations. Vague notice periods and penalty guidelines trap your firm in loss-making relationships.

Preventative Tip: Ensure your contract features a robust termination clause defining: exact notice periods, immediate termination triggers, damages, and post-termination survival liabilities.

4 Failing to Register Intellectual Property

Your trademark, domain name, marketing content, proprietary software, and product designs are intangible assets that may exceed the value of your physical property. Many businesses discover too late that their trademark has been registered by a third party, or that their former developer owns the rights to the software they funded.

Preventative Tip: Register your trademark with the Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property (SAIP) immediately upon launching. Ensure all employment and contractor agreements feature explicit IP assignment clauses.

5 Vague Founders & Shareholders Agreements

The beginning of a partnership is always harmonious — but rifts inevitably appear when allocating profits or choosing strategic directions. Without a robust Shareholders' Agreement (SHA), partner disputes can lead to forced judicial liquidation. The shareholding percentage in the Commercial Register does not automatically define voting majorities or management rights.

Preventative Tip: A Shareholders' Agreement must define: equity splits, management mandates, decision-making thresholds, profit distribution, minor shares protection, and buyout triggers. Never start a partnership without one.

6 Delayed Action on Outstanding Receivables

Outstanding commercial debt is like fire — if you don't extinguish it early, it consumes your entire cash flow. Many companies delay taking official collection actions out of courtesy or fear of losing clients. Over time, claims age and some even fall under KSA statutes of limitation, rendering them uncollectible. A bounced check is much harder to execute after 6 months.

Preventative Tip: Never allow unpaid invoices to age beyond 30 days without structured follow-up. Serve official legal warnings, then proceed to Executive Court filings. Early collections yield a much higher success rate.

7 Disregarding Regulatory Compliance & Governance

Maintaining active commercial licenses, ZATCA tax/VAT compliance, GOSI contributions, minimum wage compliance, and MHRSD internal labor bylaws are mandatory obligations. SMEs often neglect these details until they face massive compliance fines or platform freezes from the Ministry of Commerce.

Preventative Tip: Review your regulatory compliance status once every 6 months. Update employment contracts, tax filings, and trade licenses. Regulatory compliance is not a cost — it is an investment in business continuity.
