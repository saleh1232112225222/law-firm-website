Saudi Arabia Updates AML Implementing Regulations 2026 — Key Changes and Legal Implications

A comprehensive guide to the updated AML implementing regulations in Saudi Arabia: customer due diligence, suspicious transaction reporting, DNFBP compliance obligations, and the full range of administrative and criminal penalties.

Published: June 28, 2026

Saudi Arabia's Council of Ministers issued Decision No. (748) amending the Anti-Money Laundering Law, published in the Official Gazette (Umm Al-Qura), to strengthen the AML/CFT regulatory framework and align with Financial Action Task Force (FATF) international standards. This article provides a fully documented overview of the key amendments, CDD requirements, STR reporting mechanisms, DNFBP compliance obligations, and the distinction between administrative sanctions and criminal penalties.

Legislative Foundation

The updated implementing regulations are based on the Anti-Money Laundering Law issued by Royal Decree No. (M/20) dated 1/2/1439H (19 October 2017). The law was recently amended by Council of Ministers Decision No. (748), published in the Official Gazette (Umm Al-Qura). Key amendments include exempting non-profit organizations from certain obligations, amending deportation provisions in Article 28, and expanding the scope of confiscation in Article 33.

Risk-Based Approach

The Risk-Based Approach is a cornerstone of the updated regulations. Article 5 of the law requires financial institutions and Designated Non-Financial Businesses and Professions (DNFBPs) to:

  • Identify, assess, and document money laundering risks continuously, considering client-related, geographic, product, service, and delivery channel risk factors.
  • Provide risk reports to supervisory authorities upon request.
  • Assess risks associated with new products, business practices, and technologies before their deployment.
  • Adopt written policies, procedures, and internal controls approved by senior management and proportionate to the nature and scale of their business.

Customer Due Diligence (CDD) Requirements

The regulations mandate that financial institutions and DNFBPs apply Customer Due Diligence measures to all clients:

A. Client and Beneficial Owner Identification

  • Natural persons: Obtain full name, residential address or national address, date and place of birth, nationality, and verify this information using documents or data from a reliable independent source.
  • Legal persons: Obtain the entity's name and legal form, proof of incorporation, names of all directors and senior management, and registered official address.

B. Ultimate Beneficial Owner (UBO)

A UBO is any natural person who directly or indirectly owns or controls the client or exercises ultimate control. Under the implementing regulations:

  • The identity of any natural person who owns or controls 25% or more of a legal entity's shares must be identified.
  • Where no controlling stake exists, or where the identified owner is suspected not to be the UBO, the natural person exercising control through other means must be identified.
  • As a last resort, the identity of the natural person holding senior management position must be identified and verified.

C. Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)

Enhanced Due Diligence applies in high-risk scenarios including transactions with clients from high-risk jurisdictions and dealings with Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) entrusted with prominent public functions in Saudi Arabia, a foreign country, or an international organization.

Suspicious Transaction Reporting (STRs)

Article 15 — as amended — requires financial institutions and DNFBPs (including those providing legal or accounting services) to report immediately and directly to the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) when:

  • There is suspicion or reasonable grounds to suspect that funds constitute proceeds of crime.
  • A transaction is related to money laundering operations.
  • Funds are expected to be used in money laundering (including attempted transactions).

The report must include a detailed account with all available data and information about the transaction and relevant parties, along with responses to any additional information requests. Article 16 strictly prohibits tipping off the client or any other person that a report has been filed. No liability attaches to the reporter when filing in good faith.

DNFBP Compliance Obligations

Designated Non-Financial Businesses and Professions include lawyers, accountants, precious metal and stone dealers, trust service providers, and others. Their obligations include:

  • Establishing internal AML policies, procedures, and controls proportionate to the nature and scale of their business, approved by senior management.
  • Applying these policies to all branches and majority-owned subsidiaries.
  • Retaining records for at least 10 years from the date of transaction completion or account closure, or longer if required by the Public Prosecution or judicial proceedings.
  • Reporting suspicious transactions immediately without tipping off the client.

What the Amendments Mean for Lawyers

Lawyers are classified as DNFBPs under the law. The key compliance obligations include:

Obligation Details
Client Identity Verification Identify and verify the identity of natural or legal person clients using reliable documentation.
UBO Identification Determine the natural person who owns or controls the corporate client.
Record Keeping Maintain records and documents for a minimum of 10 years.
Suspicious Transaction Reporting File immediate STRs with the FIU without disclosing to the client.
Compliance Officer Appoint a compliance officer where appropriate.
Staff Training Conduct regular AML training programs for employees.

Regulatory Authorities

Authority Mandate
Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) Receive, analyze, and refer STRs to competent authorities.
Permanent Committee for AML Develop and coordinate risk-based national policies.
Supervisory Authorities Oversee financial institutions and DNFBPs and impose administrative sanctions.

Penalties: Administrative vs. Criminal

A. Administrative Sanctions (Article 25)

Supervisory authorities may impose one or more of the following for any violation:

  • Written warning
  • Compliance order with specific instructions
  • Requirement to submit regular compliance reports
  • Financial fine not exceeding SAR 5 million per violation
  • Prohibition from working in regulated sectors
  • Restriction of directors' or board members' powers
  • Suspension or removal of directors
  • Suspension or prohibition of business activity or profession
  • Suspension, withdrawal, or cancellation of license

B. Criminal Penalties (Articles 26, 27, 31)

Offense Penalty
Basic money laundering (Article 26) Imprisonment 2–10 years, fine up to SAR 5 million, or both.
Aggravated cases (Article 27) Imprisonment 3–15 years, fine up to SAR 7 million, or both (involving organized crime, violence, public office, human trafficking, or minor exploitation).
Legal persons (Article 31) Fine up to SAR 50 million but not less than double the value of the laundered funds, plus possible business suspension or office closure.

C. Additional Penalties

  • Article 28: Saudi nationals sentenced to imprisonment are banned from travel for an equivalent period; non-Saudis are deported after serving their sentence.
  • Article 33: Expanded confiscation to cover laundered funds, proceeds, and instrumentalities, regardless of whether they are in the offender's or a third party's possession.

Conclusion

The AML implementing regulations update represents a proactive step toward strengthening financial security in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia and aligning with FATF requirements. All regulated entities — particularly lawyers, accountants, precious metal dealers, and other DNFBPs — must review their internal procedures and ensure full compliance with the new CDD/EDD requirements, STR obligations, and record-keeping rules to avoid both administrative sanctions and criminal penalties.

Summary: Key AML Compliance Takeaways

  • Apply risk-based CDD measures to all clients, identifying UBOs at the 25% threshold
  • File immediate STRs with the FIU when suspicion arises — tipping off clients is prohibited
  • Maintain all records for a minimum of 10 years
  • Law firms must appoint compliance officers and conduct regular staff training
  • Non-compliance risks fines up to SAR 5M (administrative) and imprisonment up to 15 years (criminal)
  • Review internal policies immediately — the regulations are in full effect
