Legal Adaptation & Civil Liability of AI Applications under Saudi Civil Transactions Law

The world is experiencing a rapid tech revolution driven by artificial intelligence systems, which now permeate everyday life and commercial activities. This advancement raises a fundamental question: how can the legislative environment accommodate these complex technological novelties?

The flexibility of Saudi legislation is evident in the Civil Transactions Law (Royal Decree No. (M/191) of 1444H), which provides comprehensive rules and detailed provisions that enable handling the legal implications arising from the use of AI technologies.

This article is for informational and analytical purposes only and does not replace specialized legal advice.

1️⃣ Legal Classification of AI

From a strict legal perspective, AI algorithms and systems cannot be granted legal personality. The Civil Transactions Law defines persons as follows:

  • Natural person: Existence begins at live birth.
  • Legal entity: Includes the state, companies, endowments, and any entity granted legal personality by statutory provision.

Accordingly, the system classifies AI as:

  • “Non‑material thing” that can be the subject of financial rights, provided it does not exceed its nature.
  • “Property” because it represents material value used in transactions.

2️⃣ AI and Contract Formation (Automated Contracts)

Many platforms rely on AI to conclude contracts (e.g., automated trading or e‑commerce sales). The core question is: can AI possess “consent” to form a contract?

According to the law, a contract is formed by the linkage of offer and acceptance, and consent is verified by the concurrence of two legally capable parties. Since AI lacks capacity, the expression of consent is attributed to the person (programmer, owning company, or operator) who designed the system to act on its behalf.

Special Case: Contract Concluded in Error

Error TypeLegal Ruling
Gross error violating core programming (e.g., selling for zero price)Consent is null; the judge may rescind the contract and award the counter‑party only actual expenses.
Error within acceptable market fluctuations (e.g., normal price volatility)The contract remains valid and binding; compensation is the remedy for loss mitigation.

Thus, contracts concluded via pre‑programmed AI systems are considered valid and enforceable against their operators.

3️⃣ Civil Liability for AI‑Induced Damage (Tort)

The issue of civil tort liability is paramount when using AI, such as medical errors or autonomous‑vehicle accidents. The Civil Transactions Law provides clear rules applicable to these incidents.

1️⃣ Liability Based on Personal Fault

The decisive provision states: “Any fault that causes damage to another obliges the perpetrator to compensate.” If the victim proves a human error in programming, data entry, or misuse, the programmer or user is liable.

2️⃣ Liability for Things (Guardianship of AI)

The law states that “anyone who takes guardianship of objects requiring special care is responsible for any damage caused by those objects.” AI systems are classified as “objects requiring special care,” so the owner or operator bears liability without needing to prove direct fault.

The concept of “guardian” in self‑driving cars shows that guardianship extends beyond the material owner to anyone with “actual authority” over the system:

Potential GuardianReason for Liability
End‑user ownerGuardian during actual operation unless a hidden programming defect is proven.
Manufacturer or software developerGuardian if they retain authority to update or control algorithmic decisions.
Cloud‑service providerNot a guardian unless it plays a direct role at the moment of damage (e.g., signal interruption).

Compensation is apportioned according to each guardian’s degree of control.

4️⃣ Full Compensation and Exemptions

When the guardian or operator’s liability is established, full compensation is required, covering:

  • Material losses.
  • Lost earnings (provided they naturally result from the harmful act).
  • Moral damages and psychological impact.

How can an operator escape liability? They can demonstrate a break in causation by proving one of the following:

  • Force majeure or third‑party fault.
  • An unforeseeable event outside their control.
  • Unanticipated AI behavior that could not be foreseen even with the highest professional diligence (akin to force majeure).

5️⃣ Practical Case Study: Autonomous Vehicle

Facts: A Level‑4 autonomous vehicle attempts to avoid a bird, changes lane, and collides with a pedestrian, then with another person.

StepAction
1The injured pedestrian sues the “guardian” (the vehicle owner).
2The owner seeks contribution from the “manufacturer” if they did not intervene in the decision.
3The manufacturer claims “foreign cause” if the behavior was unforeseeable even by engineering standards.
4The judge obliges the mandatory insurance fund to compensate first, then refers the dispute between owner and manufacturer to experts for fault apportionment.
5If experts cannot determine liability, the damage is treated as “force majeure” unless a specific statutory provision applies.

Conclusion

The Saudi Civil Transactions Law offers a flexible legal framework that can address the technological challenges posed by AI applications. No explicit definition of AI is required; the concepts of “consent,” “guardianship of things,” and “full compensation” cover all future scenarios.

This framework provides a secure environment for technological innovation in the Kingdom while ensuring the protection of parties’ rights with fairness and rigor.
